Family Heritage: Shaher Abdulhak

A tribute to my late grandfather, my mentor and advisor, and to the values he passed on.

His greatest influence on me was not simply what he built, but how he taught me to think.

A mentor before anything else

My late grandfather, Shaher Abdulhak, built a major business enterprise from modest beginnings and became widely known as the “King of Sugar”. Those are his achievements, and they belong to him.

For me he was more than a successful businessman or a respected family patriarch. He was my mentor and my advisor. Through his teachings I learned to perfect the way I see the world: to recognise opportunities, think independently, question assumptions and look beyond immediate limitations.

Family came first

Family was central to his life. During Eid and family vacations he made it a priority to bring the entire extended family together.

It did not matter where someone lived in the world or how far they had to travel. These gatherings were an expectation, not an optional occasion. Everyone was expected to come together, spend time with one another, reconnect with relatives and keep relationships alive across generations.

We would see cousins and relatives we might otherwise go long periods without meeting. He believed in keeping the family connected, and those gatherings became an important part of our family identity.

Gathering across different places

The habit of coming together did not belong to one house or one country. Families grow and move, and he understood that staying close takes effort and a reason to meet. Photographs like this one, taken at a gathering in Cairo, are a reminder of that.

The Dewan: a place to learn and think

The Dewan was much more than a room where the family sat together. When everyone gathered, the conversation was rarely casual. He challenged people with questions across a wide range of subjects, testing what each of us knew and encouraging us to think.

No topic was too broad. Everyone was expected to take part, contribute and learn. He challenged our knowledge, encouraged curiosity and taught us to look beyond what was immediately in front of us.

Looking back, I understand that the Dewan was also his classroom. Those conversations were part of how he passed on his values and his way of thinking.

What he taught me

He taught me to recognise opportunity where others see limits, to think for myself, to question assumptions, and to look beyond the obvious. The lessons were rarely delivered as lectures. They came through questions, conversation and the example of how he lived.

His influence continues to shape how I approach business, decisions and life. The path I have built in business, from my years at Coca-Cola to the companies I founded, is my own, but the way I look at it was formed in those conversations.

His greatest influence on me was not simply what he built, but how he taught me to think.
